SAYS Happy to walk you through it! 🌿

**The core idea: a heat pump moves heat instead of making it.**
Baseboard heaters are resistance heaters. They turn electricity into heat 1:1, like a big toaster. A heat pump uses electricity to *carry* heat from one place to another, so you get more heat out than the electricity it uses.

**How it works, step by step**

A heat pump is a loop of refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature. It has four main parts:

1. **Evaporator (outdoor coil):** The cold liquid refrigerant passes through the outdoor coil. Even cold outdoor air has some heat in it, and that's enough to make the refrigerant boil into a gas.
2. **Compressor:** This squeezes the gas, which makes it much hotter. It's the part that uses most of the electricity.
3. **Condenser (indoor coil):** The hot gas releases its heat into your home's air, either through a fan unit or a water loop. As it loses heat, it turns back into a liquid.
4. **Expansion valve:** The pressure drops, the liquid gets very cold, and it goes back outside to start again.

It's the same loop your fridge uses, run in reverse. A fridge pulls heat out of a box and dumps it into your kitchen. A heat pump pulls heat out of the outdoors and dumps it into your house. In summer it can flip direction and act as an air conditioner.

**Why it's efficient**
Efficiency is measured as COP, the heat delivered per unit of electricity used. Baseboards have a COP of 1. Modern heat pumps typically get about 2 to 4, depending on outdoor temperature. That means they can use roughly a half to a third of the electricity for the same warmth.

**Things to consider for your swap**
- **Climate:** Modern cold-climate models work well well below freezing. Tacoma's mild winters are close to ideal. 🌧️
- **Ductless mini-splits:** If you have baseboards, you probably have no ductwork. Ductless mini-splits are the usual replacement. A small outdoor unit connects to one or more wall-mounted indoor units.
- **Upfront cost vs. savings:** Installation costs more than baseboards, but lower electric bills usually make up for it over time. Rebates and tax credits are often available, so check what's offered locally.
- **Keep some baseboards:** Some people leave a few as backup for the coldest snaps or for small rooms.

SAYS The difference is **when you pay tax**. 💸

- **Traditional IRA:** You may get a tax deduction now on what you put in. Your money grows tax-deferred, and you pay income tax when you withdraw it in retirement.
- **Roth IRA:** You put in money you've already paid tax on, so there's no deduction now. Your money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

**Other differences:**
- Roth IRAs have income limits for contributing. Traditional IRAs don't, though the deduction can phase out if you have a workplace plan.
- Traditional IRAs have required withdrawals starting in your 70s. Roth IRAs don't.
- You can take your Roth *contributions* (not earnings) out anytime without penalty.

**Rule of thumb:** If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket later, Roth tends to win. If you expect a lower one, traditional does. Limits and rules change yearly, so check the current IRS numbers.
