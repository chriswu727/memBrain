SAYS A heat pump doesn't make heat the way a baseboard does. It **moves** heat from one place to another, which is why it can be so efficient. 🌡️

**How it works, step by step:**

1. **Outside, it grabs heat.** A liquid refrigerant runs through the outdoor coil. Even air at -10°C holds some heat, and the refrigerant is cold enough to absorb it, so it boils into a gas.
2. **The compressor squeezes it.** Squeezing a gas heats it up, a bit like how a bike pump gets warm. Now the refrigerant is hotter than your living room.
3. **Inside, it gives heat away.** The hot gas flows through the indoor coil. A fan blows air across it, and the heat goes into your home. The refrigerant cools and condenses back into a liquid.
4. **A valve drops the pressure,** the refrigerant gets cold again, and the loop starts over.

Run the cycle in reverse and you have air conditioning, which is why these units are often called reversible.

**Why it matters for your baseboards:**
- A baseboard turns 1 unit of electricity into about 1 unit of heat. A heat pump typically gives you 2 to 4 units of heat per unit of electricity, depending on the outdoor temperature and the model. That's the big saving.
- Modern cold-climate models still work well down to roughly -15 to -25°C, so the climate usually isn't a dealbreaker.
- The catch: **a heat pump can't just be plugged in where a baseboard was.** It needs a way to deliver heat around the house. The common options are:
  - **Ductless mini-splits**, one indoor unit per room or zone. This is the most typical replacement for baseboards.
  - **A ducted system**, if you already have or can add ductwork.
  - **A hydronic (water) system**, if your home already has piping.

Your electrical panel, insulation, and the number of rooms all affect the cost and the setup, so a load calculation from a contractor is worth getting before you commit.
