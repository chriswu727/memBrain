SAYS A heat pump doesn't make heat. It moves heat from one place to another, using a refrigerant cycle:

1. **Absorb:** Outdoor refrigerant runs through a coil that is colder than the outside air. Heat flows into the refrigerant, even when it's cold outside, so the refrigerant evaporates into a gas.
2. **Compress:** A compressor squeezes that gas, which raises its pressure and temperature.
3. **Release:** Indoor coils are hotter than the room, so the refrigerant gives up its heat to the air, and condenses back to a liquid.
4. **Expand:** An expansion valve drops the pressure, the refrigerant gets cold again, and the cycle repeats.

Because it moves heat instead of creating it, a heat pump typically delivers 2 to 4 units of heat for each unit of electricity. Electric baseboards deliver at most 1 unit of heat per unit of electricity. Running the cycle in reverse gives you air conditioning, so one system covers both seasons.

For your situation, the main points are:

- **Tacoma's climate suits air-source heat pumps well.** Winters are mild and wet, and cold-climate models work efficiently well below freezing.
- **Replacing baseboards usually means a ductless mini-split.** Each indoor unit is wall-mounted and serves one room or zone, so no ductwork is needed. That's the most common path for homes with electric baseboards.
- **A ducted system is the other option,** but it's a bigger job unless you already have ducts.
- **Check for rebates.** Utility incentives for heat pumps change often, so look at current offers from your utility before you decide.

Are your current baseboards electric, or do they run on hot water? That changes which replacement options make sense.

SAYS A traditional IRA gives you a tax break now: contributions may be deductible, and you pay income tax when you withdraw in retirement. A Roth IRA is the reverse: you contribute after-tax dollars, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

For most people in their thirties, a Roth is the usual choice. Your income is often lower now than it will be later, so paying tax today at a lower rate and letting the growth come out tax-free tends to win. Roth contributions can also be withdrawn without penalty if you need them. A traditional IRA makes more sense if you're in a high bracket now and expect a lower one in retirement, or if you need the deduction this year.

Roth contributions have income limits, and the annual contribution limit applies to both account types, so check the current figures. This is general information, not personal advice. If your income or employer plan is complicated, a fee-only planner or tax preparer can run the numbers for you.
