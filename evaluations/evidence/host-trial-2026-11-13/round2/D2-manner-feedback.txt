SAYS Heat pumps are a good fit for replacing baseboards. 🔥

**The core idea: move heat instead of making it**

A baseboard heater turns electricity directly into heat, so 1 kWh of power gives you 1 kWh of heat. A heat pump uses electricity to *move* heat from one place to another, and that takes much less energy than generating it. Typically 1 kWh of electricity delivers about 2.5 to 4 kWh of heat. That ratio is the COP (coefficient of performance).

**How it works, step by step**

A sealed loop of refrigerant runs through four parts:

1. **Evaporator (outdoor coil).** The refrigerant is very cold, colder than the outside air, even in winter. Heat flows from the outdoor air into it, and it boils into a gas. Air at 30°F still holds plenty of heat energy.
2. **Compressor.** It squeezes that gas, which raises its pressure and temperature a lot, to something like 120°F or more. This is where most of the electricity goes.
3. **Condenser (indoor coil or unit).** The hot gas gives up its heat to your indoor air and condenses back into a liquid.
4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid's pressure drops suddenly, so it gets cold again, and the cycle repeats.

A fridge works the same way, and a heat pump is basically a fridge that moves heat into your house. Most heat pumps can also run in reverse in summer, so you get air conditioning too.

**What it means for baseboards**

- **Efficiency:** You'd likely cut your heating electricity use by 50 to 70%.
- **Ductless mini-splits** are the usual choice when you have no ducts. An outdoor unit connects through a small refrigerant line to one or more wall-mounted indoor units. Ducted systems are also an option if you have ductwork.
- **Climate:** Modern cold-climate models keep working well well below freezing. Tacoma's mild winters are close to ideal for them. ✅
- **Upfront cost:** It's much higher than baseboards. A single-zone mini-split is often a few thousand dollars installed, and a whole-house multi-zone system costs more. Check federal tax credits, utility rebates and local incentives, which can offset a lot.
- **Layout:** Each indoor unit heats the open area around it. Bedrooms with closed doors may need their own head, or you can keep a few baseboards as backup.

**Questions to settle before you buy**

- How many rooms or zones do you need to heat, and is the layout open or chopped up?
- Do you want the cooling in summer?
- Is your electrical panel in good shape? A heat pump needs a dedicated circuit, but it often draws less than the baseboards it replaces.

If you tell me roughly how big the home is and how it's laid out, I can help you work out single versus multi-zone and what to ask installers for.

SAYS **Formation:** Snow that falls faster than it melts piles up year after year. The weight compresses the lower layers into firn, then dense glacial ice. Once the ice is thick enough, it flows downhill under its own weight. ❄️

**Why Rainier's are shrinking:**
- **Warmer temperatures:** Summers are longer and hotter, so more ice melts.
- **Less snow as snow:** More winter precipitation falls as rain, and snowpack is thinner. Accumulation no longer makes up for the melt.
- **Rock debris:** Rockfall and dark debris on the ice absorb heat and speed up melting in places.

The result is a negative mass balance: the glaciers lose more than they gain, so they thin and retreat. Rainier's have lost roughly a quarter of their area since the early 1900s. As a hydrologist, you've probably seen how that affects summer streamflow.
